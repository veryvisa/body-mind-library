Step 2 · The main line

In one sentence

Core idea

We pick up messages about our worth from family, media and culture, often indirectly, and once we spot them we can choose to drop the ones that shrink us.

What the author is getting at

Messages from family and society shape how we feel. Becoming aware of them lets us choose which to release instead of carrying them unexamined.

What is a devaluing message?
Answer in your head, then tap to flip
A message, spoken or unspoken, that leaves you feeling less worthy or less good enough.

Can a message hurt even if the sender meant well?
Answer in your head, then tap to flip
Yes. A parent pushing for 'even better' meant encouragement, but the teen heard 'never enough'. What lands matters, even when intent was kind.

What are indirect messages?
Answer in your head, then tap to flip
Ideas absorbed from the world around you without anyone saying them to you, such as a culture valuing athletes while you are not athletic.

Notice it, write it down, decide you do not need it, and physically tear or shred the paper as a sign of the choice.

Step 5 · Four angles

Look at it from four sides

What the book says

Messages from family and society shape how we feel. Becoming aware of them lets us choose which to release instead of carrying them unexamined.

Common mix-up

A common misreading

A common wrong reading: 'If I release a message, I must be saying my family is bad.' A better one: you can love your family and still decide a particular sentence is not true about you. Some family stories are heavy, and a counselor or trusted adult is a good partner for those.

Does it still hold today?

What research says (open when you're curious)

A meta-analysis of 77 studies on media and body image found that exposure to thin-ideal images is linked to body dissatisfaction, to taking on that ideal, and to eating-related concerns, with small to moderate effects (Grabe et al., 2008). Those studies looked at women, so it only partly speaks to teen boys or to non-body messages, but it shows that messages from media are not harmless background.

  • Grabe S, Ward LM, Hyde JS. The role of the media in body image concerns among women: a meta-analysis of experimental and correlational studies. Psychol Bull 2008;134(3):460-476. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.134.3.460 link

How it connects

Activity 3 gave you the truth to compare messages against. Activity 5 replaces old messages with ones you write, Activity 11 looks at society, and Activity 24 digs into social media.
