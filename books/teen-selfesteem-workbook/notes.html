Method 1 of 6

Mind map

When to use itBefore you start an activity, to see where things go — or after, to see it all at once.
How to startWrite the topic in the middle. Add three to five branches for the big ideas. Hang short words on each branch — words, not sentences.
Five-minute versionFive-minute timer: one middle word, three branches, two leaves on each. Stop when it rings.

Method 2 of 6

Cornell notes

When to use itWhile you read or listen, when you want notes you can quiz yourself with later.
How to startSplit the page: a narrow cue column on the left, notes on the right, a summary strip at the bottom. Notes first; afterward write one question per note on the left; cover the right side and answer.
Five-minute versionRead one short section. Three notes on the right, three questions on the left, one summary sentence.

Example: Activity 6

Cues (questions)
Notes
What is compassion?
Caring about someone's struggle and wanting to help, which can include yourself.
Why does the book start with feelings for others?
Because we usually find it easy to care about a friend, a puppy, or a stranger in trouble, and that gives us words and actions to borrow.
How is self-compassion different from letting yourself off the hook?
It does not say your mistake does not matter. It says you are a person having a hard time and deserve care while you fix it.
What is a compassion letter?
A letter you write to yourself about a current struggle, using the tone you would use for your best friend.
Summary: Compassion is caring about the struggles of others and yourself, and you already know how to do it, so you can turn it on yourself with the same words and actions.

Method 3 of 6

Explain it simply (Feynman)

When to use itWhen you think you understand something but aren't quite sure.
How to startExplain it out loud or on paper as if to a younger friend, in plain words. Wherever you get stuck or reach for a fancy word, go back to the book. Then explain it again, shorter.
Five-minute versionTwo minutes explaining, two minutes checking the gaps, one minute saying it in a single sentence.

Example: Activity 6

You know how it's easy to say 'it's okay, everyone messes up' to a friend who forgot a line on stage? Self-compassion is saying that same thing to yourself. Most of us have plenty of kindness for other people and almost none left for ourselves. The book says you already know how to do it, so you borrow the words and the small actions you'd use for a friend, and point them at yourself. It isn't excusing the mistake. It's staying on your own side while you fix it.

Where I got stuck: is this just letting myself off the hook? I checked the cards: no. Compassion says I deserve care while I fix it. Now I can explain it.

Shorter, in one sentence: If I can be kind to a friend who is struggling, I can try being kind to myself the same way.

Method 4 of 6

Question chain

When to use itWhen an idea feels flat and you want to go deeper — or when you feel stuck.
How to startStart with one question. Answer it in one line. Turn the answer into the next question — why? how? what if? Go four or five links deep.
Five-minute versionOne starting question and four links, one line each.

Example: Activity 6

  1. What does it feel like when I care about a friend who is struggling?
    I want to sit with them, say something gentle and maybe do something small to help.
  2. Why can't I do the same for myself?
    Often I treat myself as if I should know better. But a friend is allowed to be having a hard time, and so am I (Activity 3).
  3. Isn't that just making excuses?
    No. Compassion doesn't say the mistake doesn't matter; it says I deserve care while I fix it (Activity 36).
  4. What would I actually say to myself?
    The same kind words I picked for a friend: it's hard, it happens to everyone, I'm still here for you.
  5. Where do I try it first?
    Write one short compassion letter about one small struggle this week, in my best friend's voice.

Method 5 of 6

Concept map

When to use itWhen the links between ideas matter as much as the ideas themselves.
How to startPut five to eight key ideas in boxes. Draw arrows between them and write a linking phrase on every arrow (shows up as, leads to, helps you…). Each arrow should read as a sentence.
Five-minute versionFive ideas, four arrows, every arrow labelled.

Method 6 of 6

One-page summary

When to use itAfter finishing an activity, or the night before a test — so you can review it in one minute.
How to startFour boxes only: the main idea in one sentence, three key points, one small picture or table, one thing I'll try. If it doesn't fit, it doesn't go in — that's the point.
Five-minute versionOne sentence, three points, a tiny sketch, one action.

Example: Activity 6

Main idea
Compassion is caring about the struggles of others and yourself, and you already know how to do it, so you can turn it on yourself with the same words and actions.

Three key points
  • Caring about someone's struggle and wanting to help, which can include yourself.
  • Because we usually find it easy to care about a friend, a puppy, or a stranger in trouble, and that gives us words and actions to borrow.
  • It does not say your mistake does not matter. It says you are a person having a hard time and deserve care while you fix it.
