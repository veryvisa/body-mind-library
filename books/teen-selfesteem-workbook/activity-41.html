Step 2 · The main line

In one sentence

Core idea

A positive decision is the one most likely to bring a good outcome, even when it is not the easiest, and it also protects how you feel about yourself.

What the author is getting at

Choices have consequences. Picking the option that is most likely to turn out well, even if harder now, raises your odds of a good result and a better opinion of yourself.

Step 5 · Four angles

Look at it from four sides

What the book says

Choices have consequences. Picking the option that is most likely to turn out well, even if harder now, raises your odds of a good result and a better opinion of yourself.

Common mix-up

A common misreading

'A positive decision is the one that feels good right now.' Often the opposite: the quick, easy or cool-looking choice can bring the bigger problem later. The better reading is to compare the outcomes, not the moods.

Does it still hold today?

What research says (open when you're curious)

A long study that followed about 1,000 children to age 32 found that childhood self-control, a close cousin of weighing outcomes before acting, predicted adult health, money and criminal-record outcomes, even with intelligence and social class taken into account (Moffitt et al., 2011). That is an association in one cohort, not proof that practicing decisions will change a life, and it is not specifically about teens' self-esteem.

  • Moffitt TE, Arseneault L, Belsky D, et al. A gradient of childhood self-control predicts health, wealth, and public safety. Proc Natl Acad Sci USA 2011;108(7):2693-2698. doi:10.1073/pnas.1010076108 link

How it connects

Activity 40 (responsibility) gives you the power; Activity 42 (facing challenges), Activity 44 (problem solving) and Activity 36 (accepting mistakes) show what to do with it. Activity 29 (peer pressure) is where the 'looking cool' pull shows up.
