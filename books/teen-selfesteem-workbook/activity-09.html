Step 2 · The main line

In one sentence

Core idea

Your family hands you roles and expectations, and noticing them is a first step toward telling which choices are really yours.

What the author is getting at

Family influences how you think, feel, and act, and it can pull you toward or away from your authentic self. Naming the roles you play is how you start to choose among them.

Step 3 · Key ideas as cards

How can family shape who you are?
Answer in your head, then tap to flip
It hands you a role (like achiever, rebel, or caretaker) and expectations. You may live up to them, please people, or push back, and these choices can change how you see yourself.
Activity 9 · Who You Are for Your Family
Can a role be both useful and not really you?
Answer in your head, then tap to flip
Yes. A role such as caretaker can help your family, yet leave little room for your own wishes. The question is whether it fits your authentic self, not whether it is good or bad.

Why can acting out be a way to get noticed?
Answer in your head, then tap to flip
If you feel small next to a sibling, trouble can bring attention, even when you do not truly want it. Seeing that link lets you look for better ways to be seen.

What is the point of imagining no expectations?
Answer in your head, then tap to flip
It separates what you do because you want to from what you do because of pressure. Some things may stay the same, and that is useful to learn too.

Step 5 · Four angles

Look at it from four sides

What the book says

Family influences how you think, feel, and act, and it can pull you toward or away from your authentic self. Naming the roles you play is how you start to choose among them.

Common mix-up

A common misreading

"My family role is just who I am." A role is something you play in a relationship, and it can be loosened. Also, parts of a role may be truly yours. If any of this feels heavy, it is okay to talk with a trusted adult or school counselor.

Does it still hold today?

What research says (open when you're curious)

Research on adolescents suggests that acting in ways that are not the real you is common, and the reasons matter. In a study of teens, those who tried out roles for experimentation reported the most positive mood and self-worth, while those who acted falsely because they felt unworthy reported the worst outcomes (Harter et al., 1996). Another line of work found that when parents' approval depended on performance, young adults felt more inner pressure and resentment and more swings in self-esteem (Assor et al., 2004). Both are correlational, mostly from US samples, and do not prove a cause.

  • Harter S, Marold DB, Whitesell NR, Cobbs G. A model of the effects of perceived parent and peer support on adolescent false self behavior. Child Dev 1996;67(2). link
  • Assor A, Roth G, Deci EL. The emotional costs of parents' conditional regard: a self-determination theory analysis. J Pers 2004;72(1). link

How it connects

This begins the "who you are for others" run, with Activity 10 (friends) and Activity 11 (society) next. It leans on Activity 8 (your authentic self) and links to Activity 4 (devaluing messages) when family words hurt.
